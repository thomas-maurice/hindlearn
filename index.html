Primer — eight things English speakers need to know

1. Aspirated vs plain

Many consonants come in pairs: a plain one and an aspirated one with a little puff of air. In English, the p in "pat" is aspirated, the one in "spat" is not — but English doesn't care about the difference. Hindi does: पल (pal, "moment") and फल (phal, "fruit") are completely different words. Aspirated letters are written with an extra h: kha, gha, chha, jha, Tha, Dha, tha, dha, pha, bha.

2. Voiced vs unvoiced

Unvoiced = vocal cords silent (k, t, p). Voiced = vocal cords humming (g, d, b). Every consonant row pairs them up.

3. Retroflex vs dental (the "two T's" trap)

Hindi has two T's and two D's:

  • Dental (त, द) — tongue touches the back of your upper teeth. Softer than English, closer to Spanish or French t/d.
  • Retroflex (ट, ड) — tongue curled back, tip on the roof of your mouth. Harder and drier. Capitalized in transliteration: Ta, Da.

English t/d sit roughly between the two — which is why native English speakers confuse them.

4. Long vs short vowels

Vowel length changes the word. इ (i) is short like "sit", ई (ii) is long like "see". Hold the long ones about twice as long.

5. The silent 'a' — schwa deletion

Every consonant carries a built-in a: क is ka, ल is la. But Hindi drops that vowel at the end of a word, and often in the middle too. So क + ल is written "ka-la" but pronounced kal. राम is raam, not "raama". घर is ghar. नमस्ते is namaste, not "namasté".

This is the single biggest gap between how the letters are named and how words are read. Rule of thumb: a word never ends in the inherent a (unless it's written with ा). Mid-word it drops too when the result is still pronounceable — समझ is samajh, not "samajha".

Sanskrit loanwords and names sometimes keep it (राम is Raam, but in Sanskrit Raama). When in doubt: drop the final one.

6. The varga (the consonant table)

Consonants are organized by where they're made in the mouth: back → front. Five rows of five, each row: unvoiced / unvoiced+aspirated / voiced / voiced+aspirated / nasal. Once you see the pattern you don't need to memorize 25 random letters — you memorize one table.

7. Matras — vowel signs (the lines above & below)

Vowels have two forms. The big standalone letters (अ आ इ ई …) are only used at the start of a word. Everywhere else, the vowel becomes a matra — a little mark that attaches to a consonant: above, below, before, or after.

So का (kaa) is not क + आ. It's क + the ा matra. के (ke) is क + a single stroke above. कै (kai) is k + two strokes above. Once you spot the pattern you can read most vowel combinations on sight — see the chart below.

8. र-blends — the disappearing र

When र combines with another consonant it doesn't write itself out as a full र. It shrinks into one of two marks: a tiny hook above the next letter (called reph) or a small diagonal stroke below the previous letter (called rakar).

The hook above means र comes first: र्क = rka, र्व = rva — you've seen it on every Indian banknote (रिज़र्व बैंक). The stroke below means र comes after: क्र = kra, प्र = pra. Same idea as the matras: a full letter gets compressed into a small attached mark.

Vowel signs — matras (मात्रा)

Each vowel has a "diacritic" form used on consonants. Tap any tile to hear the vowel sound. Pay attention to the position — that's where most of the visual logic hides.

✨ The "lines above" pattern
के = क + 1 stroke above → ke
कै = क + 2 strokes above → kai
को = क + ा + 1 stroke above → ko
कौ = क + ा + 2 strokes above → kau
One stroke = "e-ish" · Two strokes = "ai-ish" · Add the ा stick after and the vowel slides toward o / au.

Nasal marks — ं and ँ

Neither a letter nor a matra, and both unavoidable: हिंदी, मैं, माँ, अंडा all carry one. Tap an example word to hear it.

र-blends — when र attaches to another consonant

When र combines with another consonant it shrinks into a small mark. Hook on top → र comes first (reph). Stroke below → र comes after (rakar). Tap a tile to hear it.

✨ The two र-blend patterns
क्र = क + tiny diagonal stroke below → kra (base first, then र)
प्र = प + same stroke below → pra (this is the rakar)
र्क = hook above + क → rka (र first, then base — this is the reph)
र्व = same hook above + व → rva — the "rv" in रिज़र्व
Hook on top = read र first. Stroke below = read र after. That's the whole rule.

Minimal pairs — one vowel, different word

Vowel length is not decoration: it changes the word. दल is a group, दाल is lentils. Drill them in the Long vs short level. Tap the two buttons back to back — if you can reliably hear which is which, tell me and I'll turn these into listening cards too.

Look-alikes — the pairs that trip everyone up

Reading breaks on shape, not sound. These are the pairs that differ by one stroke. Tap either letter to hear it — and drill them with the Look-alikes level.

Nukta letters — the dotted ones

A dot below turns a Devanagari letter into a Perso-Arabic sound. ज़ and फ़ are everywhere in modern Hindi — ज़रूरी, फ़ोन, मेज़ — and plenty of writing drops the dot, so you also have to read them without it.

Conjuncts — stacked consonants

When a consonant has no vowel it fuses with the next one. Usually the first loses its vertical stem (क् + त → क्त); sometimes the pair becomes a shape you just learn (क्ष, ज्ञ).

Numerals ०–९ and the danda

Prices, dates, page numbers, bus routes. The full stop is । (danda), not a dot.
